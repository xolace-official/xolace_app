Variant A, refined — lifecycle, chats list, profile, ratings, specialties

Answers the two gaps: where a user finds a conversation they left, and when a new request is required versus just resuming. Adds the listener public profile with star rating and specialties, the rating prompt those scores come from, and the listener-side setup form carried over from the original mockups and extended for specialties. 11 screens. Same tokens (src/global.css), same primitives. Companion to listener-chat-mockups.html (original A) and listener-chat-variants.html (B/C/D).

Decision
A request opens a conversation once — not once per message

The accept handshake exists so a listener consents to taking this person on. That consent does not expire between messages, so re-requesting every time someone comes back would be both cold and pointless. But it does need re-checking when the listener's own availability has changed, because that is exactly what rotation, cooldown and the per-listener volume cap are protecting.

So: a conversation is permanent and always readable. Its ability to accept new messages is not. Four states, one of which (Resting) is the one the plan doc is missing.

Requested
User asked, listener hasn't answered. No messages possible yet. Auto-expires after 7 days → Declined.
Open
Accepted. Both sides message freely. Consumes one slot against the listener's volume cap.
Resting
No message from either side for 14 days. Fully readable, composer replaced by a resume affordance. Frees the slot.
Closed
Listener left the programme, or either side blocked. Read-only forever. Purged only by account deletion.
  • Resting → Open needs no new request if the listener is still active, published, and under cap. The user taps "Pick this back up", sends, and it's open again. This is the common case and it should feel like nothing happened.
  • Resting → Open needs a fresh request if the listener has gone inactive, unpublished their profile, or is at cap. The user sees why, the history stays readable, and they're offered the roster. Same copy as a decline — no-guilt, not about them.
  • The cap counts Open conversations, not lifetime ones. Without this, a listener who has ever talked to 8 people is permanently full, and the cap stops protecting anyone.
  • A user can only have one conversation per listener, ever. Requesting someone you already have history with reopens that thread rather than creating a second one — which is also how the profile screen knows to show "Open chat" instead of "Ask to talk".
  • Resting is silent. No notification, no "your conversation expired" email. The user finds out only if and when they come back, and what they find is their history plus a way to continue.
Schema implications beyond the eng review. The pending | accepted | declined field from the request/accept addition becomes requested | open | resting | closed on the conversation record, plus lastMessageAt to drive the Resting transition (a cron sweep, same shape as the existing abandoned-session job). The volume cap query filters on status: "open". The 7-day request expiry and 14-day resting window are the two tunable numbers — both are assumptions here, not researched values.

The original A mockup made the tab a directory, which left no answer to "I closed the app mid-chat, now what." A segmented control at the top fixes it in one component: Chats and Listeners. The tab opens on Chats if the user has any conversation in any state, on Listeners if they have none — so a first-time user still lands on the roster, and a returning user lands on their thread.

Screens 4–5
Listener profile — the screen nothing has specified yet

This is where the request decision actually gets made, and it's the surface the ambassador re-consent conversation is about. It shows: photo, name, bio, when they started, a star rating, and up to three specialties.

Rating a volunteer is the one thing here that can hurt the person on the other side, so it ships with three guardrails baked into the design rather than bolted on later. Nothing shows until 5 ratings — before that the profile reads "New listener", so one bad night can't brand someone. Listeners see the aggregate only, never which conversation gave which score; a 2-star from a person they spent an hour with, attributable, is the version of this feature that makes ambassadors quit. And the prompt rates the conversation, not the person ("How was this conversation?"), which is both kinder and the more honest question — a listener can do everything right on a night nothing helps.

Specialties are self-declared tags from a fixed list, capped at three — not free text, not matching. The taxonomy deliberately stops short of clinical territory: "Anxiety", "Loneliness", "Grief", "Burnout", "Family", "Relationships", "Identity", "Sleep", "Big changes". No depression, no trauma, no addiction — advertising a peer volunteer as a specialist in those invites exactly the conversations the escalation exclusion exists to keep out, and it's a claim the app can't stand behind. The same three tags power the roster's filter chips, which is what makes a directory worth browsing at all once there are more than six people in it.

The "What to expect" block is identical on every profile. It carries the non-clinical disclaimer the plan doc's constraints require, sets the response-time norm before anyone commits, and states the moderation posture plainly — the same three things a user would otherwise have to guess at.

Screen 9
Where the rating comes from

A star on a profile needs a place it was earned. This is that place: a single optional prompt shown when a conversation goes Resting, not after every message and never as a modal interrupting a live chat. Asking mid-conversation would turn an emotional exchange into a transaction; asking at the natural end of one is the only moment where the question isn't intrusive.

Skipping is free and one tap, and a skip is not a score — an unrated conversation simply doesn't count toward the denominator. Expect most to skip, which is exactly why the 5-rating display threshold matters: at six ambassadors it will take a while for anyone to show a number at all.

Screens 10–11 · listener side
Profile setup — the only way complete becomes true

Carried over from the original mockups and updated: the form now gates on four fields, not three, since specialties joined photo, name and bio. The progress meter and the Publish button both mirror the complete boolean directly — no separate confirmation step, no way to publish a half-filled profile into the directory.

The specialty picker is a fixed list with a hard cap of three. Once three are chosen the rest go disabled rather than disappearing, so the ambassador can see what they didn't pick and swap rather than wondering where the options went. Free text was rejected: it would let a volunteer advertise "depression" or "trauma" on a profile the app can't stand behind, and it makes the roster's filter chips impossible.

Consequences
What ratings and specialties change in the plan
This reverses a NOT-in-scope item. The plan doc defers "Listener specialties / RAG-based matching". Self-declared tags are the cheap half of that — no matching, no embeddings, just an array on the profile and a filter on the directory query. Worth being explicit that the deferred part (automated matching) stays deferred, so the reversal doesn't get read as reopening the whole thing.
  • listenerProfiles gains two fields: specialties: string[] (max 3, validated against a server-side enum) and the aggregate pair ratingSum / ratingCount. Store the sum, not the average — averages can't be updated incrementally without drift.
  • complete: boolean now mirrors four fields, not three. displayName, bio, photoUrl, and a non-empty specialties array.
  • A new conversationRatings row per rated conversation (conversationId, listenerId, stars, createdAt) so a rating can be audited or revoked for abuse, with a unique constraint on conversationId — one rating per conversation, editable, never additive.
  • The directory index changes. by_complete_and_active no longer covers filtered browsing; specialty filtering needs its own index or a post-filter on a bounded result set. At six listeners either is fine, but pick deliberately rather than by accident.
  • The 5-rating display threshold is a server-side rule, not a UI check. Don't ship the raw average to the client and hide it in the component — a low score on an unpublished-yet volunteer shouldn't be sitting in a network response at all.
  • Rating abuse needs an answer before launch. Six volunteers means one malicious user can meaningfully move a listener's score. Minimum viable: ratings only count from conversations with real exchanged messages, and a rating from a user who was blocked or reported by that listener is excluded.
