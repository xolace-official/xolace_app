# Prior-Art / Novelty Search: Is Xolace's Core Insight Unclaimed?

Generated 2026-08-18. Scope: has anyone else already staked this claim — academically, commercially, or in patents? A separate agent covers whether the insight is clinically *true*; this note only covers whether it is *ours*.

---

## Bottom line

- **The insight is not novel. It is a well-populated intersection.** Every one of the six claims has prior art, and two of them (naming-as-the-product, anonymous peer layer) are directly productized today by funded or Yale-backed operators.
- **The single hardest hit is claim 3.** "The person cannot yet name what they feel; naming is the product" is the explicit, published thesis of Marc Brackett's *Permission to Feel* and the operating principle of **How We Feel** — a free app co-founded by Brackett and Pinterest's Ben Silbermann, whose App Store copy says it "helps people find the right word to describe how they feel." That is Xolace's sentence, shipped, free, since 2021.
- **The most contested claim is 5 (anti-companion AI).** It was the best candidate for genuine differentiation and it is already taken at the leadership-quote level: Slingshot AI (a16z, $93M) says *"We don't want to build your best friend or your companion,"* ships session-ending and dependency monitoring in Ash. Xolace is not first here; it is second at best, and second to a much better-capitalized company.
- **The most defensible claim is 4** — targeting queue *inflow* rather than throughput — not because the idea is new (it is 1994 IOM public-health canon) but because **no consumer product currently sells it that way.** This is a category-(b) finding: known, unproductized.
- **Graveyard warning, not whitespace.** Anonymous peer support (claim 6) is not empty because nobody thought of it. Whisper is dead, Vent was absorbed and degraded, Woebot burned $124M and killed its consumer app, and Koko had to abandon consumer and become infrastructure. Occupying claim 6 means walking onto a field of headstones.

## A. Academic / clinical prior art

**Searches run:** `McGorry clinical staging model psychiatry stage 0 stage 1a subthreshold pre-clinical`; `alexithymia difficulty identifying feelings prevalence general population digital intervention app`; `emotional granularity emotion differentiation protective factor Barrett Kashdan naming emotions`; `Jorm mental health literacy "recognition" of disorders definition 1997`; `"affect labeling" Lieberman "Putting Feelings Into Words" amygdala`; `Corey Keyes languishing Adam Grant "name for the blah you're feeling"`; `Mrazek Haggerty 1994 IOM universal selective indicated prevention`; `"upstream" mental health prevention "before crisis" digital health startup positioning`.

**Finding: claim 1 is a formalized construct with at least three independent names.**

- **Clinical staging (McGorry).** Stage 0 = at-risk, asymptomatic; stage 1a = mild/non-specific symptoms; stage 1b = moderate sub-threshold. Stage 1 explicitly "encompasses the symptoms, disability and need for care that typically are below the thresholds required by current diagnostic systems." That is Xolace's gap, described in a *British Journal of Psychiatry* paper, with an intervention logic attached.
- **Languishing (Keyes 2002, popularized by Adam Grant's 2021 NYT piece).** "The absence of feeling good about your life" — explicitly the middle of a continuum between mental illness and flourishing. Grant's article was, verbatim, about giving that state a *name*. Claims 1 and 3 were already fused and put in front of tens of millions of readers five years ago.
- **IOM indicated prevention (Mrazek & Haggerty 1994).** The taxonomy exists precisely to name interventions aimed at people with detectable subclinical signs who do not meet diagnostic criteria.

**Finding: claim 3 is a named clinical construct with its own psychometric instrument and its own apps.**

- **Alexithymia** — "difficulty identifying and describing feelings" — affects roughly 10% of the general population, and "Difficulty Identifying Feelings" is a scored subscale of the TAS-20. Xolace's stated user is a clinical construct with decades of literature.
- It is also already a *product* target: **MT-ALEX** (smartphone emotion-recognition skills training, RCT pilot) and a 2026 *Digital Health* paper on "a mechanism-based digital intervention for alexithymia" with vocabulary training and expressive writing as core components.
- **Affect labeling** (Lieberman et al. 2007) is the mechanism: naming an emotion reduced amygdala response and increased RVLPFC activity. Brackett compresses this to "if you can name it, you can tame it."
- **Emotional granularity** (Barrett; Kashdan, Barrett & McKnight 2015) is the trait version, and the literature already says interventions to improve it "can both reduce psychological problems and increase various strands of well-being."
- **Mental health literacy** (Jorm 1997) covers the recognition half — "the ability to recognize specific disorders" — but is about *disorders*, not *feelings*. This is the one academic frame that genuinely does **not** cover Xolace: Jorm's recognition is diagnostic self-labeling, not affective granularity. Partial coverage only.

**Honest read:** the academic layer covers claims 1 and 3 completely, claim 4 conceptually, and claim 6 as an intervention modality. Nothing in Xolace's framing is unknown to the field. What the field has *not* done is turn it into a consumer product with a coherent tone.

## B. Commercial prior art — direct (naming/identifying, not treating)

**Searches run:** `"Ahead app" emotional intelligence app positioning "name your emotions" App Store description`; `Rosebud journal app positioning "AI journal" tagline`; `app "you don't know what you're feeling" positioning mental health app tagline`; plus direct fetch of the How We Feel App Store listing.

**How We Feel — the real competitor for claim 3.** Own words, App Store: an "emotional wellbeing journal," "a free app created by scientists, designers, engineers, and therapists to help people better understand their emotions," which "helps people find the right word to describe how they feel," built around a "color-coded matrix" (the Mood Meter), "conceived in conjunction with Yale University's Center for Emotional Intelligence and based on the work of Dr Marc Brackett." Free, non-profit, Yale-credentialed, Pinterest-cofounder-built. Category (d): productized well, real competitor — for claim 3 specifically.

Where How We Feel is weak: it is a *taxonomy picker*, not a conversation. It assumes you can locate yourself on a grid. The person who "doesn't know what they feel" may not be able to pick a tile either. That is a real gap — but it is a gap in *execution*, not in positioning.

**Ahead** — "The Duolingo for emotional intelligence," Apple Design Award 2024 (Social Impact), ~2M users. Positioning is skill-building for anger/anxiety/stress management, and "an emotional profile that shows the root of how you react." Adjacent, not the pre-naming layer: it assumes you know you have an anger problem.

**Rosebud** — "your personal AI-powered self care companion," chat-based journaling that "asks questions, carries a conversation, remembers patterns." Note the word: *companion*. Rosebud is the direct antithesis of claim 5 and confirms that the default AI-journaling posture is relational.

**Daylio / Journey / Stoic / Reflectly / Mindsera / Finch** — all downstream of naming. Daylio is a mood tracker (you pick a face). Finch is an explicit virtual-pet companion. None claims the pre-naming layer.

**Verdict on B:** claim 3 is category (d) against How We Feel, and category (c) against everyone else.

## C. Commercial prior art — adjacent (AI mental health, and the anti-companion test)

**Searches run:** `Slingshot AI Ash therapy app positioning "not a companion" anti-sycophancy design against dependency`; `Headspace Ebb "AI companion" description Wysa positioning "emotionally intelligent" own words`; `Limbic Access AI triage NHS talking therapies`; `Woebot shut down consumer app 2025 reasons`.

**(i) Does anyone claim the pre-naming layer?** No. Every product surveyed enters after the user has a label:

- **Limbic Access** — the purest inverse. AI triage/assessment for NHS Talking Therapies, Class IIa UKCA medical device, 650k+ assessments, ~45% of NHS Talking Therapies, "assessment time reduced by 23.5%, saving 12.7 minutes per referral." This is *throughput optimization*, stated in throughput metrics. It is the perfect foil for claim 4 — and it validates that claim 4's framing is genuinely unoccupied on the consumer side.
- **Ash (Slingshot AI)** — "the first AI designed for therapy." Therapy, i.e. downstream.
- **Wysa** — "an emotionally intelligent chatbot that uses AI to react to the emotions you express." Note *you express*: presupposes expression.
- **Headspace Ebb** — "empathetic AI companion," "trained in motivational interviewing." MI is a change-readiness technique; it presupposes an identified target behavior.
- **Woebot, Youper, Sanvello, Happify, Bloom, Intellect, Calm, Spring/Lyra/Modern Health** — all CBT-delivery, content-delivery, or care-navigation. All post-naming.

**(ii) Does anyone design *against* companionship?** Yes — one, and it is the most dangerous finding in this note.

**Slingshot AI**, leadership quoted directly: *"We don't want to build your best friend or your companion."* Ash reportedly "ends conversational sessions after it deems a certain amount of progress is made," and the team "built in the ability to monitor for dependency and change the conversation to reduce the chances it becomes a problem." $93M from a16z, Radical, Forerunner, Felicis, Menlo; 50k-user beta before a July 2025 public launch; they have published a real-world study addressing over-reliance and internalization head-on.

Everyone else leans in: Headspace calls Ebb a *companion* in its own headline; Rosebud calls itself a *companion*; Wysa's own research blog claims "emotional bonds with AI digital therapeutic Wysa are equivalent to human therapist relationships" — the literal opposite of the campfire metaphor. Replika and Character.ai are companionship by definition.

**Verdict on C:** claim 5 is **not** first-mover territory. It is a two-horse position, and the other horse is better funded and has clinical-study output. What Xolace still holds is the *combination*: anti-parasocial design applied at the pre-clinical, pre-naming layer rather than at the therapy layer. Narrow, but real.

## D. Anonymous peer layer — mostly a graveyard check

**Searches run:** `Supportiv anonymous peer support matched by struggle "not a social network"`; `anonymous peer support app shutdown Koko Whisper Vent "shut down" moderation cost safety`; `7 Cups criticism listener quality TalkLife pivot Koko Elephant HearMe status 2025`; `Vent app shut down TalkLife acquired Vent`.

**Live and close:** **Supportiv** — asks "What's your struggle?", uses AI-driven NLP to match into moderated small groups of ≤5 peers, "no app to download, no PHI collected, no registration or signup." That is claim 6, shipped, with a B2B/public-sector revenue model. Category (d).

**Also live:** 7 Cups (volunteer listeners, 30–60 min training — persistent quality complaints), TalkLife (~$3.65M raised over 7 rounds; "real-time professional moderation" and clinical escalation), HearMe (seed, pivoted to B2B).

**The graveyard:**

| Product | Fate | Known reason |
|---|---|---|
| Whisper | iOS app dead 2020, Android 2022, platform non-operational | Anonymous-network unit economics and safety load |
| Vent | Acquired by TalkLife; users report it was degraded post-acquisition | Consolidation, not product-market fit |
| Woebot (consumer) | Shut down June 2025, 1.5M users cut off, ~$124M burned | FDA has a pathway for rule-based chatbots but none for LLM-based ones; company pivoted to enterprise/clinical licensing |
| Koko | Sold to Airbnb, relaunched as a **nonprofit**; now sells moderation infrastructure to TikTok/Snap/OpenAI rather than running a consumer destination | Consumer peer support did not sustain as a business |

**Read this carefully:** the two organizations that got furthest in anonymous peer support (Koko, Woebot) both concluded that the consumer destination was not the business — one became a nonprofit, one became enterprise software. Supportiv, the healthiest survivor, sells to employers and the public sector, not to consumers. If Xolace's peer layer is a consumer-retention feature, that is defensible; if it is expected to be the business, four precedents say otherwise.

## E. Patents and IP

**Searches run:** `patent "emotional state" matching users similar emotional state anonymous peer support system USPTO`; `Google Patents conversational agent identifying labeling user emotion natural language mental health triage patent`.

Nothing found that plausibly constrains this design. What exists is adjacent and mostly narrow:

- **US10,728,393** (Intuit) — emotion recognition to match *support agents* with customers; requires facial-recognition data. Not applicable.
- **US10,835,168** — estimating/predicting emotional states with real-time feedback; contemplates anonymous operation. Broad-sounding but about state estimation, not peer matching.
- **US12,436,597** — "emotional pattern matching system": classifies current user emotional state and matches it *to an emotional therapy*, not to another user.
- **US11,120,895B2** — mental health assessment via semantic analysis/NLP; distinguishes "mental states" from transient feelings.
- Univ. of Michigan — speech-based longitudinal emotion/mood recognition.

**Plain statement:** I found no live patent that reads on (a) conversational AI that helps a user *name* an emotion, or (b) matching users to anonymous peers by emotional similarity in a non-social-graph product. Freedom to operate looks fine. Also: nothing here to file on defensively — the concepts are too well-published to be novel for patent purposes.

## F. Investor / market framing

**Searches run:** `Rock Health digital behavioral health market map categories 2025 2026 subclinical prevention`; `a16z thesis AI mental health consumer market map 2025`; `Y Combinator request for startups mental health 2025 2026 RFS emotional wellbeing`.

- **Rock Health** added "AI chatbots for mental health" as a *new* category to its 2025 Innovation Maturity Curve. Mental health is the top-funded clinical indication for the seventh consecutive year — but the funding narrative is "delivery infrastructure needed to make behavioral health services more **scalable**, like provider marketplaces." That is throughput. **No category label exists for the naming/pre-clinical layer.**
- **a16z** — no published "naming layer" thesis surfaced; their mental-health bet is Slingshot AI, i.e. AI *therapy*.
- **YC RFS 2026** — 15 categories, none mental-health-specific (AI-native services, vertical agents, space, energy, synbio, defense). No RFS to point at.
- Generic "upstream mental health" language exists in public-health advocacy (AAAS, MedCity) with the standard ROI stat — every $1 in prevention returns $2–$10 — but it is framed at the community/school/policy level, not as a consumer product category.

**Verdict on F:** claim 4 has no category label in any market map. That is the one place where "nothing found" is genuinely encouraging — the concept is legible to funders (upstream prevention is a known good) but nobody has claimed the label commercially.

## Whitespace vs graveyard

**Genuine whitespace (unclaimed, no known failed attempts):**
- Consumer positioning around *queue inflow* rather than throughput (claim 4). Not in Rock Health's map, not in a16z's thesis, not in YC's RFS. Known in public health, absent in product.
- Anti-parasocial design applied specifically at the *pre-clinical* layer. Slingshot occupies anti-parasocial-at-therapy; the pre-clinical slot is open.

**Graveyard (unclaimed because it was tried and abandoned):**
- Consumer anonymous peer support (claim 6). Whisper dead; Vent absorbed; Koko → nonprofit + infrastructure; Woebot consumer → dead after $124M; Supportiv and HearMe → B2B. Reasons on record: LLM regulatory ambiguity (Woebot), moderation and safety load, and consumer unit economics that never closed.
- Free/nonprofit competition in the naming layer (claim 3). How We Feel is free and Yale-backed. That is not a graveyard but it is a price ceiling: the closest analogue to Xolace's core promise costs $0.

**Neither — just crowded:** claims 1, 2, 3. Well-populated, well-funded, well-published.

---

## What is actually defensible

Stated as narrowly and honestly as the evidence allows, Xolace can claim:

> A consumer product that meets a person *before* they have a word for what they feel, resolves that into a name through conversation rather than a taxonomy picker, does so with an AI explicitly engineered against parasocial attachment, and reflects back anonymous strangers who arrived at the same name — sold on reducing how many people escalate into the care queue rather than on moving the queue faster.

Every clause of that has prior art. **The composition does not.** No single actor holds all four: How We Feel holds naming but is a grid and has no AI and no peers; Slingshot holds anti-parasocial but is therapy; Supportiv holds anonymous peers but is B2B and has no naming layer; Limbic holds the queue but is optimizing the wrong end of it.

That is a defensible *product*, not a defensible *insight*. Nobody should pitch claims 1 or 3 as discoveries — Keyes named the state in 2002, Adam Grant put it in the NYT in 2021, and Brackett wrote the book and shipped the app. Pitching them as new is the fastest way to lose credibility with a mental-health-literate investor, of whom there are many after seven straight years of mental health being the top-funded indication.

The claims worth leading with are 4 and 5, in that order, and both should be stated as *positioning choices*, not as insights nobody has had.
